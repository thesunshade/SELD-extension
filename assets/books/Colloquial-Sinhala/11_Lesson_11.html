3. THE THIRD PERSON PRONOUN (meaa) SET

Another set of location words is used as an equivalent of the third person pronoun. This set, with the usual distinction in location, is:

meaa

he, she, this person

oyaa

area

he, she, that person (near you)

eaa

he, she, that person (over there)

he, she, that person (in question)

The plural forms are made by adding -la: meaala, oyaala, areala, eaala 'they'. meaa will serve as a model for the case forms of the entire set:

Direct

meaa

Dative

meaaTa

meaagen

All four members of the set are used as third person singular pronouns for equals and inferiors. They are not generally used for superiors. In addition to this use, one of the set, i.e., oyaa is also used as a second person pronoun to address intimate equals:

oyaa enəkoTə, kaar-eken enna.

When you (intimate) come, come by car.

[page 151]

ohee enekoTe, kaar-eken enne.

When you (more formal) come, come by car.

Here as with other second person pronouns, no simple rule can be given for when it may be used, since such usage differs from group to group and area to area. In general, it is safer not to use oyaa as a second person pronoun unless the person addressed is a very intimate friend.

4. LIKE, LIKE TO kæməti

With kaməti 'like', the equivalent of the English subject is in the direct case, and the object is in the dative case. kæməti takes the assertion marker, and as with other forms in i , the result is ii . Thus the form is kamətii when it is not followed by an item like da or nææ :

are mahattea ee pattareeTə kæmətii.

That gentleman likes that newspaper.

eaa osTin-ekəkətə kamati da?

Does he like an Austin?

Note that with oona 'want’ the English subject is in the dative case whereas with kæməti it is in the direct case. The English object with oona is in the direct case whereas with kaməti it is in the dative case:

mama mee potəTə kamətii.

I like this book.

maTə mee pote onnə.

I want this book.

kæməti may have a dependent verb in the infinitive, with or without its own object and modifiers:

mahattea uDeraTə næTun balanna kaməti də?

Do you like to see up-country dances?

siigiriyəTə yanakoTə mamə ee taanaayamee nawatinnə kamatii.

When I go to Sigiriya, I like to stay in that rest house.

ohee are kaDee eloolu gannə kæməti də?

Do you like to buy vegetables at that shop?

The negative of kaməti is formed by adding name. Thus:

mahattea are potaTə kaməti naeee.

The master doesn't like that. book.

mahattea oya suruTTuwaTə kæmeti nædda?

Don't you like that cigar?

[page 152]

5. t ekka 'WITH'

‘With’, indicating accompaniment, is expressed by the post-position-tekke. The t is added to the preceding noun (in the direct case) and ekka follows:

putaat ekkə

with the son

lameat ekka

with the child

When the preceding noun ends in a consonant (as when it is in the indefinite) thet is preceded by -u-:

lameekut ekkə

with a boy

mahatteekut ekka

with a gentleman

mame plus -t may appear as maat. Hence: maat ekke ‘with me’.

6. issərəha ‘IN FRONT OF’

The postposition issərəha ‘in front of’ takes the direct case:

geeTTuwe issərəha

in front of the gate

kaDee issərəha

in front of the shop

etənə isseraha

in front of that place

7. ‘AT WHAT TIME?’

The Sinhalese expression for ‘at what time’ is kiiyəTə də?

‘At about what time’ is kiiyəTə witərə də?

In the corresponding answers (’at nine’, etc.), the dative case of the numeral is used:

nameeTa

at nine

nameeTə witərə

at about nine

Similarly:

nameeTə witərə ennə puluwan də?

Can you come at about nine?

mama enne pahate witərə.

I am coming at about five.

(Note that witərə in these expressions takes no assertion marker or indefinite ending since it is witare ‘about’, not witərə ‘only’).

8. kataa kərənəwa

kataa kərənəwa may mean either ‘call’ or ‘speak’. As ‘call’ it takes the dative case of the person called:

[page 153]

taatta maTə kataa kərənəwa.

Father is calling me.

mahatteaTə heTə ram kataa kərannə.

Call the master tomorrow night.

As ‘speak’ it takes the direct case:

mame dæn sinhələ kataa kərənəwa.

I speak Sinhalese now.

mehee kataa karanne sinhələ witəray.

They only speak Sinhalese here (i.e., ‘only Sinhalese is spoken here’).

It may also be used with t ekke in the sense ‘talk with’:

maTə mahatteat ekkə Tikak kataa kərannə onnə.

I want to talk a bit with the gentleman.

ehenan lameat ekkə kataa karanna.

If so, talk with the boy.

9. kiyənəwa

kiyənəwa with an infinitive has the sense ‘tell (someone) to’.

Thus:

lamea yanakoTə maTə kataa kərannə kiyannə.

Tell the boy to call me when he is going.

This sentence means ‘Tell the boy now that he should call me when he is going. Note that lamea is the subject of yanəkoTə and not the object of kiyanne. When the person told to do some-thing is expressed in Sinhalese, it is in the dative case. Thus:

lameaTə maTə kataa karannə kiyanne.

Tell the boy to call me.

Similarly:

mahattea siigiriyəTə yanakoTə ee taanaayəmee nawətinnə kiyanna.

Tell the gentleman to stop at that rest house when he goes to Sigiriya.

Note that mahattea is the subject of yanekoTe, as in lamea yanekoTə above. Compare:

mame lameaTə weDe karannə kiyənəwa.

I am telling the boy to work.

[page 154]

Here lameaTə directly expresses the person told, and is not the subject of waDə kəranne.
