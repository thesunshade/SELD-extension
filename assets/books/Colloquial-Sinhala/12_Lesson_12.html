3.2 The use of the wi form is illustrated by:

pahaTə witərə gedərə eewi

(He) is likely to come home about five.

nattan mahattea koheewat yaawi.

Otherwise, the master is likely to go somewhere.

dæn mahattea eewi.

The master will probably come now.

lamea irida koləmbəTə yaawi.

It is likely that the boy will go to Colombo on Sunday.

The wi form indicates future time and implies some lack of certainty. There is no single English equivalent that will translate this form under all circumstances, so that it may be variously rendered by such equivalents as 'may, might, will probably, is likely to', depending upon context. In general, the implication of the wi form is similar to that of English 'it is likely that', or 'will probably'.

The wi form may be either second or third person, singular or plural, but it occurs as second person only in rare instances. It is never first person.

In questions, də immediately follows the wi form:

eaa ade rame naTumak balanna yaawi də?

Is he likely to go to see a dance tonight?

4.2 Thennan form of a verb refers to an event in the future, and is used only with a first person subject, either singular or plural, implied or stated.

It may be used as a polite way of asking permission:

mamə yannan?

May I go?

api ennan?

May we come?

Another use of this form is as a polite way of agreeing to do something, or saying that you will do something if it is all right with the person you are talking to:

mama kiyannan.

I'll tell him (if that's what you want me to do).

Tælifoon karannan.

I'll telephone.

api pahaTə ennan.

We'll come at five (if it's all right with you).

4.3 Sentences with nnan verbs can never be questions formed with the question marker də. They do not have negatives.

5. THE CONJUNCTION nan 'if'

The conjunction nan 'if', like the question marker də may, in a simple statement, occur after the simple verb. In an emphatic statement it may occur after the emphasized item, but not after the emphatic form of the verb. Also like də, it may occur after onnə, puluwan and kæməti in sentences with these forms, or after the predicate attribute in equational sentences.

8. TELLING TIME

8.1 In asking the time at any given moment (as opposed to at what time something will occur or has occurred--see Lesson 11) any one of several expressions involving kiiyə də may be used:

welaawe kiiyə də?

What time is it (literally, How much is the time)?

den welaawe kiiyə də?

What time is it now?

dæn kiiyə də?

What time is it now?

The order may be changed with no change in meaning:

kiiyə də welaawe?

kiiyə də dæn welaawa?

kiiyə də dæn?

8.2 In answering the questions above, used with the appropriate numeral: the assertion marker is

ekay 'one (o'clock)'

or dæn ekay 'It's one (o'clock) now.'

dekay 'two (o'clock)' or den dekay 'It's two (o'clock) now.'

9. wenawa IN TIME EXPRESSIONS

9.1 When used with a specific hour of the day, wenewa implies that it will be that time when something else occurs:

mahattea enakoTə haya wenawa.

It will be six when the mas-ter comes.

koocciyə yanakoTə aTə wenewa.

It will be eight when the train goes.

If wenekoTə is used with a specific time, it implies that something will happen by that time:

mahattea haya wenəkoTə enewa.

The master will come by six.

koocciyə aTə wenəkoTə yanawa.

The train will go by eight.

9.2 rase wenawa means either 'getting towards night' or 'getting well into the night', depending upon context. Thus it may equate with either 'be late in the afternoon' or 'be late at night'.

næTun iwərə wenəkoTə rasee wenǝwa də?

ræse wenawa.

Will it be late at night when the dances end?

On some days it's late in the afternoon when (he) comes home.

10. taama AND tawe

While taame and tawe may both be rendered by English 'still' or sometimes 'yet', there is a clear distinction between them.

taamə

indicates that something is as it has been in the past.

tawa

implies that there is still some left, or still some distance to go. Thus:

mahattea taamə kantooruwe.

lamea taamə koləmbəTə yanawa.

The master is still at the office.

The boy is still going to Colombo ('as he has been' or 'he hasn't reached there yet').

But:

tawe huŋ̆gak welaa tiyenawə.

tawe hetəpmə pahak tiyenawə.

There is still much time left.

There are still five miles (to go).
