Notes on the Conversation

Anuradhapura was the first capital of the Sinhala kings, and the period of Sri Lankan culture up to about the 10th century A.D. is known as the “Anuradhapura Period.” The ruins of many ancient structures, both secular and religious, remain from this period and demonstrate the remarkable engineering and organizational skill of those who constructed them. Although many of these structures remain in ruins, some of the religious edifices, ancient shrines, and great irrigation works have been restored. Today, some of the latter are again in use, and the restoration work continues steadily.

[page 60]

Sigiriya is situated about 50 miles to the south of Anuradhapura. There, a large rock was transformed into a royal palace during the 5th century. Originally, entrance to the palace was gained by means of a stairway cut inside the rock, and the stairway was entered through the mouth of a large sculptured lion hence the name Sigiriya ‘Lion Rock’. The most famous feature for visitors to Sigiriya today are the magnificent frescoes depicting beautiful women in a gallery on the rock face. These date from the 5th century, and there are a number of poems commenting upon them which were written on a wall below by admiring visitors of the 8th through 10th centuries: the famous “Sigiri Graffiti”.

taanaayəmə තානායම ‘resthouse’: Rest houses are maintained either by the Government Tourist Bureau or by the government of the area in which they are located. They provide food and lodging at a moderate rate, and are found in almost any important town. One may go to a rest house for a meal or for tea without taking a room. Increasingly, these are going “upscale” or being replaced by tourist hotels, however.

hooTələ හෝටල ‘hotel’: In Sinhala or Sri Lankan English, a hotel is not necessarily a place to stay. The term is extended as well to restaurants of almost any type.

3. THE INDEFINITE IN TIME PHRASES

In expressions of periods of time, just as in expressions of distance, price or other quantities, Sinhalese commonly uses the indefinite of a quantity phrase unless a particular stretch of time is referred to, or the form of the sentence is such that the assertion marker is required:

[page 62]

anuraadəpuree dawas kiiyak nawətinne oonə də?
අනුරාදපුරේ දවස් කීයක් නවතින්නෙ ඕන ද ?
How many days should (one) stay in Anuradhapura?

dawas dekak nawətinnə.
දවස් දෙකක් නවතින්න .
Stay two days.

or the answer could be:

dawas dekay.
දවස් දෙකය් .
Two days.

4. INFINITIVES WITH NOUNS

In Sinhalese an infinitive form may modify a noun just as an infinitive with ‘to’ may in English, but the Sinhalese infinitive precedes the noun:

balannə tæn
බලන්න තැන්
places to see

nawətinnə hooTəleak
නවතින්න හෝටලයක්
a hotel (in which) to stay

Like any other noun, a noun so modified may be used in the plural as part of a quantity phrase:

balannə taaŋ huŋ̆gak
බලන්න තාං හුඟක්
many places to see

nawətinnə hooTələ tunak
නවතින්න හෝටල තුනක්
three hotels to stay in

5. THE SINHALESE WRITING SYSTEM

The Sinhalese writing system, known as the siŋhələ hooDiyə සිංහල හෝඩිය, ‘the Sinhalese alphabet’ has 54 basic symbols used in the literary tradition. These 54 include 18 vowel symbols and 36 consonant symbols. For writing spoken Sirihalese, however, only 36 symbols are necessary. These include 24 basic consonant symbols and 12 vowel symbols.

Each symbol is called akurə අකුර ‘the letter’, pl. akuru අකුරු. There is no modern language other than Sinhalese for which the sinhələ hooDiyə සින්හල හෝඩිය is used.

5.1 Consonants

In the Sinhalese writing system, a consonant symbol by itself represents a consonant plus a following vowel, which will be called THE INHERENT VOWEL. This inherent vowel will be ə අ or a අ depending upon position within the word. The first symbols to be learned are the following:

ක k ə (or ka)

ද də (or da)

හ h ə (or ha)

ට T ə (or Ta)

ම m ə (or ma)

ර r ə (or ra)

ද D ə (or Da)

ස s ə (or sa)

ල l ə (or la)

ත t ə (or ta)

ග g ə (or ga)

ව wə (or wa)


Note that some symbols are quite different in form while others such as ට, ම and ව or ග and හ are similar in their basic shape.

The following rules for the inherent vowel will apply in almost all cases:

1. In words written with a single consonant symbol, the inherent vowel will be ə අ. Thus the question marker is ද.

2. A consonant symbol at the beginning of a word will have the inherent vowel a අ.

3. A consonant symbol at the end of a word will have the inherent vowel ə අ.

For the two-syllable words written with two consonant symbols, then the effect of rules 2 and 3 together is that the first vowel will be a අ, the second ə අ.

රස rasə ‘tasty’

මට maTə ‘I, me’, (dat.)

මම mamə ‘I’

කඩ kaDə ‘shops’

4. In the middle of words longer than two syllables, the inherent vowel will be a except under certain regular conditions to be learned later and in some irregular cases:

දවස dawəsə ‘the day’

දවසක dawəsəkə ‘on a day’

හතර hatərə ‘four’
